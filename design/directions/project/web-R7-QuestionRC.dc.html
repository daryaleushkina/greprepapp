For decades, ecologists assumed that city trees suffered mainly from air pollution. A recent survey of street trees in twelve cities points to a different culprit: heat. Pavement and buildings absorb sunlight by day and release it at night, so busy streets can stay several degrees warmer than nearby parks. Trees growing beside wide roads showed signs of drought stress even after heavy rain, while trees of the same species in shaded courtyards did not. The authors do not dismiss pollution, but they argue that planting programs should favor heat-tolerant species and that cities should measure surface temperature before choosing where new trees are likely to survive.
